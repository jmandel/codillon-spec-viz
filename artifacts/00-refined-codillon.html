Refined Codillon

Codillon is Keith Winstein’s structured editor for WebAssembly, written for Stanford’s CS 10N. You type one instruction per line. On every keystroke Codillon re-validates and re-runs your module, and it draws what it knows in the margin. Each value’s type is a shape, the operand stack is a set of curves from producer to consumer, anything wrong is darkred, and a slider lets you travel through the run. Refined Codillon adds one idea: a type is a set of values, and a refinement is a smaller set drawn inside the type’s shape. With that idea the editor checks more than types. It checks that your module does what its spec says, and when it doesn’t, it shows you where and why in the same margin, with the same shapes. This page composes the strongest devices from ten competing prototypes into one design. The prototypes, the research and the synthesis are in the study.

How to read this page: every picture is live. Drag a slider or press ← / → to step through a run. Hover a shape to magnify it, and click to pin the magnifier (on a touch screen, tap a shape to magnify it and tap again to pin). Click a loop line or a function header to open a lens. When the editor suggests a fix, click it, and the code changes. Undo puts it back.

1 · A type is a set

An i32 has 2³² possible values. Codillon already draws it as a rectangle, and in Refined Codillon that rectangle is the number line. INT_MIN sits at the left wall, INT_MAX at the right wall, and 0 in the middle. The scale is linear from −8 to 8 across the middle 60% and logarithmic out to the walls, so small numbers and huge ones both stay readable. A refinement (i32 | P) carves out the region where P holds. Fence posts mark bounds: a filled cap is inclusive and a hollow cap is exclusive. A comb marks parity. Sockets mark a finite set. A dashed tether ties a bound to some other value’s dot. Choose a predicate and move the value.

Six states, one cup

The checker gives every obligation one of six verdicts. Each verdict has its own look, and they stay distinct in grayscale. Darkred always means violated and nothing else. There is no green: proven looks like nothing at all. Below, the same cup requires 0 ≤ v < 8 and is fed 1 ≤ v ≤ 8, so the value 8 spills over the rim. The six panels are six things the checker can say about that spill.

2 · Reading a line

Every instruction already has a fraction: its inputs are cups above a rule, and its outputs are solid shapes below it. In Refined Codillon the fraction is a sequent. Facts known about the inputs sit above the rule, facts claimed about the outputs sit below it, and the rule itself is the verdict. Here $next adds 1 to an index in [0, 8) and hands it to $slot, which requires [0, 8). The sum can be 8, so the cup on line 8 spills, and the editor inserts a hole after it that holds the counterexample. Move the input, hover the rows, or accept the suggestion.

3 · Lesson 1 · parity

$half accepts only even numbers. Its parameter is (i32 | $x % 2 == 0), a precondition: every caller must prove it, and the body may assume it. $half_or_neg1 earns the fact with a gate. Line 15 negates is_even(x), and the if on line 16 sends odd numbers to the then-arm. In the else-arm $x is known to be even, so the comb on line 20 fits the cup on call $half (line 21) tooth for tooth. In the clean program the margin stays quiet.

Seed P1 deletes the negation. Now the else-arm is reached by odd numbers. The comb arriving at line 21 has its teeth on the odd values, and those teeth spill over the rim. The editor inserts a hole after the call that holds the witness x = 3, and it marks ⚖ twice: on line 21, where the obligation fails, and on line 15, the line you must change. Replay the witness, then accept the code chip. The hole closes. Seed P3 is a spec bug: “half is smaller” is false for 0 and for every negative even number. Nothing in the body spills. The hole attaches to the header’s second conjunct, and its only chip is a spec chip. The rule is blame goes where the fix goes.

4 · Lesson 2 · integer square root

$isqrt searches upward for the largest r with r·r ≤ n. A student usually gets there in four steps. Each tab below is one step of the same listing, and accepting a hole’s first chip moves you to the next tab.

  1. No invariant yet. At the top of the loop the checker knows nothing about $r. It finds a loop-head state, n = 3 with r = 5, that leaves through the exit with a wrong answer. The loop line shows ∞? because nothing says the loop ends. The chip moves the invariant into lines 7–8.
  2. Signed attempt. The invariant holds, but the comparison on line 17 is i32.gt_s. With r = 46340 the product on line 15 is 46341² = 2147488281. That number is past INT_MAX, so the product’s shape grows a nub beyond the wall, and a dotted arc shows where the machine wraps it to −2147479015. The cup on line 17 reads the product signed, and the nub spills. A real run would first fail at step ≈695 000, so the counterexample is a one-iteration replay from that state. Replay it, then click the product shape on line 15 to open the ring.
  3. No lemma hint. With i32.gt_u the remaining question is nonlinear: does r·r ≤ n give r ≤ 46340? The checker can’t tell. The hole is indigo and unknown, never darkred, and it counts how often the run-time check held (∘ 4). It offers an apply? list. The wrong instance shakes; (@use sq_lt_sq $r 46341) closes the hole. You can also admit the fact instead, and then everything the admission supports is ghosted.
  4. All proven. The listing is calm. Click the loop line for the lap chart: every lap is ivory and the exit cell is proven. Click the header for the relation plot. Then seed S2, the spec bug, and every perfect square lights up.

5 · Lesson 3 · a hash table

Eight slots in linear memory at address 65440, each 12 bytes holding a state (EMPTY, FULL, TOMB), a key and a value. Collisions use linear probing, and deleting a key leaves a tombstone. The spec talks about a (@ghost global $M), an abstract map from keys to values. It exists only for the checker and is erased from the binary. The definitions st(s), key(s) and val(s) read memory as a typed array of slots. The representation invariant R ties memory to $M, and its five clauses sit as chips on the module line. Every exported function requires and ensures R. Mid-operation R may be false: insert writes the state, key and value one store at a time. The mirror lens draws that as gray “R open”, never as darkred.

Seed (b) makes $find stop at a TOMB as if it were EMPTY. R stays true the whole time. The hole sits at $find’s early return, and its strip shows key 11 one slot past the TOMB. The suggestions fork. You can fix the exit test, which says your code is wrong. Or you can strengthen (iii) to what line 38 assumes, which says your spec is short. Take the second: the hole moves to delete’s store and is false there, because no delete can keep the stronger (iii). Seed (d) lets the eighth insert through. The ring closes, $find probes forever, the lap chart’s measure staircase drops below zero, and the hole holds the corrected clause (iv). Seed (a) drops the mask on the probe index: when you replay it, the run hits 💣 at line 142 before it ever reaches the ⚖ on 151. A bomb outranks a scale.

6 · What the checker hands the editor

The checker never stops at the first error. It returns a residue, which is a list of obligations, and each picture above is drawn from one. Here is the record behind the signed-attempt hole in Lesson 2. The meaning is Lean’s: each surface construct maps to a Lean notion, so a hole is literally a goal Lean would ask you to close.

7 · Rules we kept

  

    
Six states

    
Gutter precedence
💣 trap › 𝄞 malformed › ⚖ spec refuted › ✗ invalid › 𝄽 hole / unknown › 12 number

      
⚖ goes on the line where the obligation fails and on the line you must change, when those differ. Hollow ⚖ means the witness hasn’t been replayed yet; solid means it has.

    
Mid-operation discipline
An invariant may be false between checkpoints: loop-head arrivals, operation boundaries, and writes to a refined local. Misalignment in between is drawn gray as open, never darkred. Only a checkpoint can fail.

    
Quiet by default
Lenses (the ring, the lap chart, the mirror, the relation plot) open only when you click. There is no green anywhere. Darkred means violated and nothing else. Every darkred is one click from a witness on the slider. Proven is silence: a gray line number.

  

  
Limits

  
    
    
  • Mocked checker. Every verdict, hole, witness and suggestion is written out by hand from the fixture (03-sample-programs.md) for each reachable state. No solver runs on this page. The runs are real: a small line interpreter executes each listing, and it reproduces the fixture’s key frames (see the footer).
    • 
    
  • Sizes chosen, not tested. 84 × 14 px for a refined i32 (the spec said 72 × 12, but the run-time values inside were unreadable at that size), the 60% linear middle, 14 px chips and the hole layout were picked by eye at 1400 px wide. None of them has been tried with students.
    • 
    
  • Left out: fact ribbons as an always-on layer (04). They cost too much ink at 30 px rows. What survives of them is the causal trail: dashed lanes from a hole back to the lines its hypotheses came from, in darkred for the line to blame. The envelope strips (09) are out because the ring and the lap chart already tell the overflow story. The sampler grids for the hash table (08) are out because the mirror carries Lesson 3. Proof wires (unsat cores drawn as curves) are left out entirely.
    • 
    
  • Mixed syntax. Lessons 1 and 2 use the 01 syntax ($x binders, named results). Lesson 3 shows the fixture’s hash-table text verbatim, which binds the result as v.
    • 
    
  • Folding. Lesson 3 folds function bodies that hold no hole, and folds R’s five clause lines into chips on the module line. Codillon has no folding today.
    • 
    
  • The S1 witness. The one-iteration replay starts at n = 2³¹−1, r = 46340, as in the fixture. 01 §5 instead quotes n = 2147395600, r = 46339 for its own version of the listing.
    • 
    
  • Two departures from the spec. The ⚖ glyph is line art, so a filled ⚖ and an outlined ⚖ look the same in grayscale. Here ⚖ sits in a badge instead: a solid darkred disc when the witness has been replayed, a dashed ring when it hasn’t. An unknown 𝄽 also carries a small “?”, because in grayscale it was indistinguishable from a false one. And the mirror docks in the lens column beside the listing, under the slider, rather than across the full width. A full-width mirror would push the code off the screen.
