Refined Codillon

Codillon is Keith Winstein’s structured editor for WebAssembly, written for Stanford’s CS 10N. You type one instruction per line. On every keystroke Codillon re-validates and re-runs your module, and it draws what it knows in the margin. Each value’s type is a shape, the operand stack is a set of curves from producer to consumer, anything wrong is darkred, and a slider lets you travel through the run. Refined Codillon adds one idea: a type is a set of values, and a refinement is a smaller set drawn inside the type’s shape. With that idea the editor checks more than types. It checks that your module does what its spec says, and when it doesn’t, it shows you where and why in the same margin, with the same shapes. This page composes the strongest devices from ten competing prototypes into one design. The prototypes, the research and the synthesis are in the study. How the design runs, and is proven, in the real editor is in Refined Codillon in the real editor.

How to read this page: every picture is live. Drag a slider or press ← / → to step through a run. Hover a shape to magnify it, and click to pin the magnifier (on a touch screen, tap a shape to magnify it and tap again to pin). Click a loop line or a function header to open a lens. When the editor suggests a fix, click it, and the code changes. Undo puts it back.

1 · A type is a set

An i32 has 2³² possible values. Codillon already draws it as a rectangle, and in Refined Codillon that rectangle is the number line. INT_MIN sits at the left wall, INT_MAX at the right wall, and 0 in the middle. The scale is linear from −8 to 8 across the middle 60% and logarithmic out to the walls, so small numbers and huge ones both stay readable. A refinement (i32 | P) carves out the region where P holds. Fence posts mark bounds: a filled cap is inclusive and a hollow cap is exclusive. A comb marks parity. Sockets mark a finite set. A dashed tether ties a bound to some other value’s dot. Choose a predicate and move the value.

Six states, one cup

The checker gives every obligation one of six verdicts. Each verdict has its own look, and they stay distinct in grayscale. Darkred always means violated and nothing else. There is no green: proven looks like nothing at all. Below, the same cup requires 0 ≤ v < 8 and is fed 1 ≤ v ≤ 8, so the value 8 spills over the rim. The other five panels are five things the checker can say about that spill. The first panel, proven, is fed 1 ≤ v ≤ 7 instead, which fits.

Names in predicates. A parameter or a named result is referred to by its own name ($x, $root). The bare v in the picker above, and in the hash table’s headers (the fixture’s text, kept verbatim), names the value being refined. Inside a hole, v₁₅ names the value produced on line 15.

2 · Reading a line

Every instruction already has a fraction, drawn just left of its text: its inputs are cups above a rule, and its outputs are solid shapes below it. In Refined Codillon the fraction is a sequent. Facts known about the inputs sit above the rule, facts claimed about the outputs sit below it, and the rule itself is the verdict. Here $next adds 1 to an index in [0, 8) and hands it to $slot, which requires [0, 8). The sum can be 8, so the cup on line 8 spills, and the editor inserts a hole after it that holds the counterexample. Hover the hole to see its causal trail: dashed lanes, drawn to the right of the code, back to the lines its facts came from (darkred for the line to blame). Move the input, hover the rows, or accept the suggestion.

3 · Lesson 1 · parity

$half accepts only even numbers. Its parameter is (i32 | $x % 2 == 0), a precondition: every caller must prove it, and the body may assume it. $half_or_neg1 earns the fact with a gate. Line 15 negates is_even(x), and the if on line 16 sends odd numbers to the then-arm. In the else-arm $x is known to be even, so the comb on line 20 fits the cup on call $half (line 21) tooth for tooth. In the clean program the margin stays quiet.

Seed P1 deletes the negation. Now the else-arm is reached by odd numbers. The comb arriving at line 21 has its teeth on the odd values, and those teeth spill over the rim. The editor inserts a hole after the call that holds the witness x = 3, and it marks ⚖ twice: on line 21, where the obligation fails, and on line 15, the line you must change. Replay the witness, then accept the code chip. The hole closes. Seed P2 swaps i32.shr_s for i32.shr_u, which is right for every even x ≥ 0 and wrong for every negative one. Seed P3 is a spec bug: “half is smaller” is false for 0 and for every negative even number. Nothing in the body spills. The hole attaches to the header’s second conjunct, and its only chip is a spec chip. The rule is blame goes where the fix goes.

4 · Lesson 2 · integer square root

$isqrt searches upward for the largest r with r·r ≤ n. A student usually gets there in four steps. Each tab below is one step of the same listing, and accepting a hole’s first chip moves you to the next tab.

  1. How a loop is checked. The verifier cuts the program at loop $grow. It treats any state that satisfies the (@invariant) as a possible arrival at the loop head, whatever the code before the loop did, and it checks one iteration from there. So a counterexample can be a loop-head state that your run never reaches. With no invariant every state qualifies, even r = 5 when line 3 sets r = 0.
  2. No invariant yet. At the top of the loop the checker knows nothing about $r. It finds a loop-head state that leaves through the exit with a wrong answer: n = 3 with r = 5 in the preview, and n = 0 with r = −5 from Z3 in the live editor. The loop line shows ∞? because nothing says the loop ends. The chip moves the invariant into lines 7–8.
  3. Signed attempt. The invariant holds, but the comparison on line 17 is i32.gt_s. With r = 46340 the product on line 15 is 46341² = 2147488281. That number is past INT_MAX, so the product’s shape grows a nub beyond the wall, and a dotted arc shows where the machine wraps it to −2147479015. The cup on line 17 reads the product signed, and the nub spills. A real run would first fail at step ≈695 000, so the counterexample is a one-iteration replay from that state. Replay it, then click the product shape on line 15 to open the ring.
  4. No lemma hint. With i32.gt_u the remaining question is nonlinear: does r·r ≤ n give r ≤ 46340? The design assumes a checker that can’t tell. (Z3 in the live editor proves it outright, so there the line is simply silent.) In the preview the hole is indigo and unknown, never darkred, and it counts how often the run-time check held (∘ 4). It offers an apply? list. The wrong instance shakes; (@use sq_lt_sq $r 46341) closes the hole. You can also admit the fact instead, and then everything the admission supports is ghosted.
  5. All proven. In the live editor this is a real proof. Every obligation goes to Z3 and every line is silent. In the preview below the listing is calm too. Click the loop line for the lap chart: every lap is ivory and the exit cell is proven. Click the header for the relation plot. Then seed S2, the spec bug, and every perfect square lights up.

5 · Lesson 3 · a hash table

Now live in the editor. The real editor executes the ghost map $M and checks the representation invariant R on every run. It also proves the table with Z3, one operation at a time from any state that satisfies R, so no call sequence is needed. The clean table is proven, all 54 obligations, which takes about 30 s in the browser. Pick a seed and the live embed at the end of this section shows the real failing run, matching the fixture step for step. Each seeded bug also comes with the solver’s counterexample, drawn as an 8-slot card with $M. Still preview: the hole lines and their suggestion chips, and the memory-mirror and lap-chart lenses.

Eight slots in linear memory at address 65440, each 12 bytes holding a state (EMPTY, FULL, TOMB), a key and a value. Collisions use linear probing, and deleting a key leaves a tombstone. The spec talks about a (@ghost global $M), an abstract map from keys to values. It exists only for the checker and is erased from the binary. The definitions st(s), key(s) and val(s) read memory as a typed array of slots. The representation invariant R ties memory to $M, and its five clauses sit as chips on the module line. Every exported function requires and ensures R. Mid-operation R may be false: insert writes the state, key and value one store at a time. The mirror lens draws that as gray “R open”, never as darkred.

Seed (b) makes $find stop at a TOMB as if it were EMPTY. R stays true the whole time. The hole sits at $find’s early return, and its strip shows key 11 one slot past the TOMB. The suggestions fork. You can fix the exit test, which says your code is wrong. Or you can strengthen (iii) to what line 38 assumes, which says your spec is short. Take the second: the hole moves to delete’s store and is false there, because no delete can keep the stronger (iii). Seed (d) lets the eighth insert through. Line 11 already holds the corrected clause (iv), with used < CAP as its last conjunct, so the hole sits after line 166 and says that conjunct breaks. Replay it: the run stops at step 488, where the eighth insert returns with the ring closed. The run then never terminates. Drag the slider to the end, unfold $find, and click loop $probe: the lap chart’s measure staircase drops below zero. If you take the hole’s spec chip, which drops used < CAP, you get the other view of the same bug. The hole moves into $find’s back-edge as a termination failure, and its chip offers the corrected clause (iv) back. Seed (a) drops the mask on the probe index. The first probe of insert(15,150) computes i = 8, so the back-edge invariant fails at step 143 (⚖ on 151). The next call $slot fails its requires at step 145 (⚖ and a spill on 140). The load on 142 then traps at step 153 with address 65536. Replay lands on the trap, and 💣 takes line 142’s gutter, because a bomb outranks a scale on the same line.

6 · What the checker hands the editor

The checker never stops at the first error. It returns a residue, which is a list of obligations, and each picture above is drawn from one. In the real editor that residue is real. Each Obligation in src/vc.rs carries its site, kind and goal, and gets a Verdict: proven, refuted with a model, or undecided. The verdict for every obligation in every example is checked in at src/testdata/proof_verdicts.txt. Below is the same kind of record for the signed-attempt hole in Lesson 2, written out with the extra fields the design adds (edit site and suggestions). The meaning is Lean’s: each surface construct maps to a Lean notion, so a hole is literally a goal Lean would ask you to close.

7 · Rules we kept

  

    
Six states

    
Gutter precedence
💣 trap › 𝄞 malformed › ⚖ spec refuted › ✗ invalid › 𝄽 hole / unknown › 12 number

      
⚖ goes on the line where the obligation fails and on the line you must change, when those differ. Hollow ⚖ means the witness hasn’t been replayed yet; solid means it has.

    
Mid-operation discipline
An invariant may be false between checkpoints: loop-head arrivals, operation boundaries, and writes to a refined local. Misalignment in between is drawn gray as open, never darkred. Only a checkpoint can fail.

    
Quiet by default
Lenses (the ring, the lap chart, the mirror, the relation plot) open only when you click. There is no green anywhere. Darkred means violated and nothing else. Every darkred is one click from a witness on the slider. Proven is silence: a gray line number.

  

  
Limits

  
    
    
  • What is real. The editor embedded in §0, §3, §4 and §5 is a fork of Codillon (github.com/jmandel/codillon, branch refined). These parts are real:
      
      
        
    • It parses the spec syntax, erases it from the binary, and checks every refinement, @requires, @ensures, @invariant, @decreases, @assert and the hash table’s ghost map and R on the actual run.
      • 
        
    • It sends every obligation to Z3, a single-threaded WebAssembly build that runs in a worker. Proven lines are silent. Refuted lines get ⚖ and the solver’s counterexample state. A function-entry counterexample replays on the slider from a dashed track, and a hash-table state is drawn as an 8-slot card with $M. Undecided lines get an indigo ?. Across the examples that is 419 obligations: 398 proven, 20 refuted, 1 undecided.
      • 
        
    • Deep links (#ex=, #src=, step=) open the exact states embedded here.
      • 
      
    
      Still design preview: the hole lines with their suggestion chips (accept, undo, fork), and the ring, lap, mirror and relation lenses. The preview listings are driven by a small line interpreter that reproduces the fixture’s key frames (see the footer).
      Known costs: the hash table takes about 30 s to prove in the browser; the main thread pauses briefly while it generates obligations; and cubes stays undecided, because the encoding treats large products as beyond 64 bits. The live embeds need the page to be served next to editor/; opened from a file, they link to the deployed editor instead.
    • 
    
  • Sizes chosen, not tested. 84 × 14 px for a refined i32 (the spec said 72 × 12, but the run-time values inside were unreadable at that size), the 60% linear middle, 14 px chips and the hole layout were picked by eye at 1400 px wide. None of them has been tried with students.
    • 
    
  • Left out: fact ribbons as an always-on layer (04). They cost too much ink. What survives of them is the causal trail: hover or focus a hole and dashed lanes, drawn to the right of the code, run back to the lines its hypotheses came from, in darkred for the line to blame. The envelope strips (09) are out because the ring and the lap chart already tell the overflow story. The sampler grids for the hash table (08) are out because the mirror carries Lesson 3. Proof wires (unsat cores drawn as curves) are left out entirely.
    • 
    
  • Mixed syntax. Lessons 1 and 2 use the 01 syntax ($x binders, named results). Lesson 3 shows the fixture’s hash-table text verbatim, which binds the result as v.
    • 
    
  • Folding. Lesson 3 folds function bodies that hold no hole, and folds R’s five clause lines into chips on the module line. Codillon has no folding today.
    • 
    
  • The S1 witness. The one-iteration replay starts at n = 2³¹−1, r = 46340, as in the fixture. 01 §5 instead quotes n = 2147395600, r = 46339 for its own version of the listing.
    • 
    
  • Checked against the real editor. We built upstream Codillon at commit 005a19f, ran it, and matched its listing geometry. Fractions sit just left of each instruction, at Codillon’s dataflow-driven indent (25 px steps, with frames adding 3). Connections are its 9 px muted ribbons. Frames are rounded brackets, pink for loops, with a small open circle once a loop has been taken. The current line gets its chevron-tailed navy arrow. The slider has numbered ticks and darkblue and darkred pills. A trapping line turns darkred, with its commentary label at the far right. The fonts are its MLModern fonts. Refined shapes stay on the left at 84 × 14, and a function’s code moves right only as far as its widest refined fraction needs. Everything this study adds lives to the right of the code: holes, fact chips, causal trails. Still different from the real editor: an if frame is gray here (Codillon draws if green and else purple, and this page has no green). The ◂ ▸ buttons, the ghost track and the run band are additions. Our spec lines use Codillon’s comment style, darkblue condensed sans. Run-time values in shapes are 9 px rather than Codillon’s 8 pt, for legibility.
